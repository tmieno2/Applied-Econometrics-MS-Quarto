09: Endogeneity

Endogeneity

Transcript

Endogeneity is the central threat to causal interpretation in an OLS regression. The zero-conditional-mean assumption says that, after conditioning on every included explanatory variable, the remaining error should have mean zero. When that condition fails, at least one regressor contains information about the unobserved part of the outcome. OLS then mixes the effect of that regressor with the effect of whatever is hiding in the error term. The four labels at the bottom are common routes to this problem. They overlap conceptually: selection is often an omitted-variable problem, and reverse causality is another mechanism that creates correlation between a regressor and the error.

Endogeneity

E[u\mid x_1,\dots,x_k] \ne 0

The zero-conditional-mean assumption fails: the error term is systematically related to at least one explanatory variable.


Endogenous independent variable

If an explanatory variable x_k is, for whatever reason, correlated with the error term, we call x_k an endogenous explanatory variable.


Sources of endogeneity

  • Omitted variable
  • Selection
  • Reverse causality
  • Measurement error

Omitted Variable

Transcript

Use the wage equation to see exactly where omitted-variable bias comes from. The true model includes education, experience, and ability. If ability is unavailable and we leave it out, it does not disappear; its contribution moves into the composite error, which I call v. Education is likely related to ability, so education is now correlated with v. The estimated education coefficient therefore captures both the return to education and part of ability’s association with wages. The sign of the bias depends on two signs: how ability affects wages and how ability is related to education. This is why merely saying that a variable was omitted is not enough to determine the direction of bias.

True Model

\log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 exper + \beta_3 ability + u


Incorrectly specified (your) model

\log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 exper + v, \qquad v=u+\beta_3 ability

Transcript

This sensor example turns the abstract definition into a research design. We would like beta one to measure how adopting a sensor changes irrigation. But these are observational data: farmers decide whether to adopt. Ask what the error term contains—soil, management skill, water prices, conservation preferences, and many other determinants of irrigation that are not in this simple equation. If any of those factors also influence adoption, sensor and u will be correlated. Before running a regression, the useful habit is to describe the decision that generated the explanatory variable. That decision often reveals the likely confounders.

Research Question

Does a soil moisture sensor reduce water use for farmers?


Data

Observational (non-experimental) data on soil moisture sensor adoption and irrigation amount


Model of interest

irrigation = \beta_0 + \beta_1 sensor + u

  • irrigation: the amount of irrigation by the farmer
  • sensor: indicator equal to 1 if the farmer adopted a soil-moisture sensor


Question

Is sensor endogenous (is sensor correlated with the error term)?

Transcript

Think of adoption as an outcome with its own equation. Farmers combine observed and unobserved information to decide whether a sensor is worthwhile. Possible inputs include soil type, farm size, crop mix, water cost, prior drought experience, technical ability, and conservation preferences. Now compare that list with the determinants of irrigation demand. The overlap is the problem. For example, high water prices can make a sensor more attractive and independently reduce water use. A farmer’s conservation preferences can encourage adoption and reduce irrigation even without the technology. Those common causes make adopters and non-adopters systematically different before we ever observe their water use.

Farmers do not adopt soil-moisture sensors randomly. They use available information to decide whether adoption would benefit them.


Adoption (selection) equation

sensor = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x_1 + \dots + \alpha_k x_k + v


Question

What variables might farmers consider when deciding whether to adopt a soil-moisture sensor?


Question

Do any of the variables listed above also affect irrigation demand?

Transcript

Soil type is a concrete common factor. Sandy soil drains quickly, which can raise irrigation requirements. The same feature can make real-time moisture information more valuable, raising the probability of sensor adoption. If soil type is not measured, its effect on irrigation sits in the regression error. Because sensor adoption also responds to soil type, the sensor indicator becomes correlated with that error. Notice that this argument does not require farmers to make mistakes or researchers to use bad data. Rational self-selection into treatment is enough to generate endogeneity in an observational comparison.

Example

Soil quality/type (hard to accurately measure)

  • Farmers whose fields are sandy may be more likely to adopt a soil-moisture sensor.
  • Sandy fields may require more irrigation.


Key

Soil quality or type affects both sensor adoption and irrigation.

  • sensor is a function of soil quality/type
  • irrigation is also a function of soil quality/type, which enters the error term when it is not controlled for
Transcript

Here is the same argument written directly in the outcome equation. The parenthetical expression is the composite error when soil type is omitted. Sensor adoption varies with soil type, and soil type appears inside that composite error, so the covariance between sensor and the error is not zero. OLS cannot distinguish water use caused by the sensor from water use associated with different soils. Depending on the relationships, the estimated sensor effect can be too positive, too negative, or even have the wrong sign. The important conclusion is not just that adopters differ; it is that they differ along a factor that also affects the outcome.

\begin{aligned} irrigation = \beta_0 + \beta_1 sensor + (\beta_s\,\text{soil type}+v) \end{aligned} where v includes all other unobserved determinants of irrigation.

Note

So, sensor and the error term in the irrigation model are correlated through soil type, leading to biased estimation of the impact of a sensor.

Transcript

Selection bias can sometimes be addressed by controlling for the variables that jointly determine treatment and outcome. If soil type is measured well and included in the irrigation equation, its contribution no longer sits in the error term. Comparisons are then made among farms with the same included soil measure. But the conclusion is conditional: this fixes the problem only if the measure is accurate, the functional form is adequate, and no other relevant confounders remain. Adding controls mechanically is not a guarantee of causality. We need a substantive argument that treatment is as good as randomly assigned after conditioning on those controls.

Note

Selection bias is a form of omitted variable bias.


If you accurately measure the common factors in the two equations, you can simply include them explicitly in the main model.

For example,

\begin{aligned} irrigation = \beta_0 + \beta_1 sensor + \beta_s\,\text{soil type} + v \end{aligned}

If soil type is measured accurately and there are no other confounders, including it removes this source of correlation between sensor adoption and the error term.

Reverse Causality

  • Context
  • Treatment decision
  • Reverse causality
  • Another example
Transcript

Now consider treatment and health. The simple cross-sectional regression compares currently treated and untreated patients. Doctors did not assign treatment randomly, and current health is not a before-and-after change. The error contains severity, prognosis, access to care, and other determinants of current health. Because those factors can also affect who receives treatment, a raw comparison may make an effective treatment look ineffective—or harmful—simply because the sickest patients are most likely to receive it. This is the classic reason observational medical comparisons require a careful account of the treatment-assignment process.

Research Question

Does a particular type of medical treatment improve health?


Data

Observational (non-experimental) cross-sectional data on a particular type of medical treatment and current health. Treatment is chosen by doctors rather than randomly assigned.


Model

health = \beta_0 + \beta_1 treatment + u

  • health: measure of a patient’s current health
  • treatment: indicator equal to 1 if the patient receives treatment

This model compares the health of treated and untreated patients at a point in time; it does not make a before-and-after comparison.


Question

Is treatment endogenous? (Is treatment correlated with the error term?)

Transcript

Doctors use health information when choosing treatment. In the stylized selection equation, health helps predict treatment; if poorer health means a greater chance of treatment, alpha one would be negative when higher values mean better health. The equation is intentionally simple, but the logic is general. Treatment status reflects information about the outcome, including information the analyst may not observe. That makes treated and untreated patients incomparable without additional assumptions or a different design. Keep the timing straight: if doctors respond to baseline health, this is selection on a pre-treatment condition. If current health and current treatment jointly influence each other, we have simultaneous or reverse causality.

Question

How do doctors decide whether to place their patients under medical treatment?

Answer Their patients’ health conditions.


Selection (treatment decision) model

treatment = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 health + v

Less healthy people are more likely to be treated.

Transcript

These two equations show a feedback system. Treatment affects health in the first equation, while health affects treatment in the second. A health shock contained in u changes health and thereby changes treatment. Treatment is consequently related to u, violating the condition needed for OLS in the health equation. Calling the problem reverse causality highlights that the arrow is not only from treatment to health; it also runs from health to treatment. In an application, the exact label depends on timing. A treatment response to earlier health is selection on baseline health, while contemporaneous feedback is genuine simultaneity. Either way, the one-equation OLS coefficient is not automatically causal.

Consequence

\begin{aligned} health &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 treatment + u,\\ treatment &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 health + v. \end{aligned}

A shock in u changes health, and health in turn affects treatment. Consequently, treatment is correlated with u and is endogenous in the health equation.


Reverse Causality

This endogeneity problem is called reverse causality: the proposed outcome also affects the explanatory variable of interest. If treatment is chosen using pre-treatment health instead, the problem is more precisely described as selection on baseline health.

Transcript

Environmental enforcement provides another setting where policy responds to the outcome it is supposed to improve. Regulators observe discharge quality and target inspections, penalties, or other actions toward facilities with serious violations. We then observe facility-level outcomes and enforcement over time. A naive regression asks whether facilities receiving more enforcement have better water quality. But those facilities were often selected precisely because their water quality was worse. The institutional rule that directs enforcement is therefore part of the econometric model, not background detail.

Context

  • Under the Clean Water Act, facilities that discharge waste into water (e.g., oil refineries) must comply with applicable discharge requirements.

  • The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) can take enforcement actions (e.g., financial penalties) against facilities that violate those requirements.

Research Question

Are enforcement actions effective in improving the quality of wastewater discharges?


Data

Annual data on

  • wastewater-discharge quality measures for individual facilities
  • enforcement actions taken against facilities by the EPA
Transcript

The two arrows are explicit here. Enforcement may improve water quality, which is the causal effect we want. At the same time, poor water quality triggers enforcement. As a result, enforcement actions are correlated with unobserved shocks to water quality. A negative association could mean enforcement harms quality, but it could also mean regulators correctly target deteriorating facilities. Even a positive association can be difficult to interpret without knowing the targeting process. We need exogenous variation in enforcement, a credible panel design, or another identification strategy to separate policy response from policy impact.

Model of Interest

\text{water quality} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{enforcement actions} + u


Selection (enforcement decision) model

\text{enforcement actions} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \text{water quality} + v

  • water quality is affected by enforcement actions
  • enforcement actions are affected by water quality

Consequence

Enforcement actions are endogenous because they respond to water quality itself.

Measurement Error

  • What is it?
  • ME in the Dependent Variable
  • ME in Independent Variables
Transcript

Measurement error means the recorded variable is not the underlying quantity the model intends to use. Survey respondents may round or misreport income, farmers may recall yields imperfectly, and spatial interpolation gives an estimate rather than the exact weather at a field. The consequences depend critically on which variable is measured with error and how that error is related to the true variable, the regressors, and the structural disturbance. There is no general rule that measurement error always biases every coefficient toward zero. We will first examine error in the dependent variable and then the classical case for an explanatory variable.

Definition

The observed value of a variable differs from its true value.


Examples

  • reporting errors (any survey has the potential for misreporting)
    • household survey on income and savings
    • survey on rice yield by farmers in developing countries
  • the use of estimated values
    • spatially interpolated weather conditions (precipitation)
    • imputed irrigation costs

Question

What are the consequences of measurement error in variables used in a regression?

Transcript

Start with the true outcome y-star and define the recording error e as observed minus true. Substituting the observed outcome into the model simply adds e to the original disturbance. OLS remains unbiased and consistent if that measurement error has conditional mean zero given all regressors. Intuitively, the observed points become noisier around the same conditional mean, so estimates become less precise but do not systematically tilt. This is a condition, not an automatic result. If reporting error varies systematically with x—for example, high-income households underreport by more—the composite error can be correlated with the regressors and coefficients can be biased.

True Model

y^*= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_k x_k + u

with the relevant OLS assumptions satisfied, including E[u\mid x_1,\dots,x_k]=0.


Measurement error

Define the difference between the observed value y and the true value y^* as

e = y-y^*


Estimable Model

Using y=y^*+e, the estimable model is

y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_k x_k + v, \qquad v=u+e


Question

What are the conditions under which OLS estimators are unbiased?


Answer

E[e\mid x_1, \dots, x_k] = 0

Together with E[u\mid x_1,\dots,x_k]=0, this implies E[v\mid x_1,\dots,x_k]=0. Measurement error in the dependent variable then increases noise but does not bias the OLS coefficient estimators.

  • Classical errors-in-variables (CEV)
  • Direction of bias
Transcript

Now the explanatory variable is measured imperfectly. The true model uses x-star, but we only observe x, which equals x-star plus error. Solving for x-star and substituting produces an estimable equation in x with a composite error v equal to u minus beta one times e. This is the key difference from outcome measurement error: the same e appears both inside the observed regressor and inside the new error term. The OLS requirement is therefore immediately in danger. We next impose the classical assumptions to see whether that seemingly favorable case solves the problem—it does not.

True Model

Consider the following general model

y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1^* + u

with the relevant OLS assumptions satisfied.


Measurement error

Define the difference between the observed value x_1 and the true value x_1^* as

e_1 = x_1-x_1^*


Estimable Model

Using x_1^*=x_1-e_1, the estimable model is

y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + v, \qquad v=u-\beta_1 e_1


Question

OLS requires E[v\mid x_1]=0. Even when u is exogenous, this condition fails if the observed regressor x_1 is correlated with its measurement error e_1. Does classical measurement error create such a correlation?

Transcript

Classical measurement error is mean zero and uncorrelated with the true regressor and the structural error. Even under those strong assumptions, the observed x contains e by construction. Its covariance with e therefore includes the variance of e, which is positive whenever measurement error exists. Because v contains minus beta one times e, observed x is correlated with v. This is the endogeneity result. It can feel surprising: e is unrelated to the true x, yet it is necessarily related to the recorded x because the recorded value literally includes it.

Definition

Under classical errors-in-variables assumptions, the measurement error has mean zero and is uncorrelated with both the true regressor and the structural error:

E[e_1]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x_1^*,e_1)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(u,e_1)=0.

Even though e_1 is uncorrelated with x_1^*, it is correlated with the observed regressor x_1=x_1^*+e_1:

\begin{align*} \operatorname{Cov}(x_1,e_1) &=\operatorname{Cov}(x_1^*+e_1,e_1)\\ &=\operatorname{Cov}(x_1^*,e_1)+\operatorname{Var}(e_1)\\ &=\sigma_{e_1}^2>0. \end{align*}

Thus, under the classical assumptions, the mismeasured regressor is correlated with the composite error v=u-\beta_1e_1.

Transcript

In the simple-regression classical case, the probability limit equals the true coefficient multiplied by the reliability ratio lambda. Lambda is the variance of the true signal divided by total observed variance, signal plus measurement-error noise. It lies between zero and one, so the estimate is pulled toward zero. A positive coefficient is biased downward; a negative coefficient is biased upward, meaning less negative. More measurement-error variance produces more attenuation. Remember the scope of this result: it is the classical, single-mismeasured-regressor case. With nonclassical error, multiple regressors, or several mismeasured variables, the direction need not be toward zero.

Question

So, what is the direction of the bias?


Direction of bias

Under classical measurement error,

\operatorname{plim}(\widehat{\beta}_1) =\beta_1\frac{\operatorname{Var}(x_1^*)} {\operatorname{Var}(x_1^*)+\operatorname{Var}(e_1)} =\lambda\beta_1, \qquad 0<\lambda<1.

  • If \beta_1>0, the asymptotic bias is negative.
  • If \beta_1<0, the asymptotic bias is positive.


Attenuation bias

  • The coefficient estimator is inconsistent and converges toward zero relative to the true coefficient.

  • More measurement-error variance makes the attenuation factor \lambda smaller.
