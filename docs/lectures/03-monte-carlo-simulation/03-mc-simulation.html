Monte Carlo Simulation: Introduction

Lecturer transcript

Monte Carlo simulation is how we settle arguments in this course when the algebra gets hard to trust. The idea is simple: instead of proving something, you build a fake world where you already know the answer, then check whether the method recovers it. Read the three bullets. The first is confirming theory we already proved, which is what we do today. The second is the one I actually use in research, when I suspect a problem in my data and no theorem covers it. The third is why it is in this lecture: numbers are easier to believe than Greek letters.

It is a way to test econometric theories via simulation.


How is it used in econometrics?

  • confirm econometric theory numerically
    • OLS estimators are unbiased if E[u|x]=0 along with other conditions (theory)
    • I know the above theory is right, but let’s check if it is true numerically
  • You kind of sense that something in your data may cause problems, but there is no proven econometric theory about what’s gonna happen (I used MC simulation for this purpose a lot)
  • assist students in understanding econometric theories by providing actual numbers instead of a series of Greek letters
Lecturer transcript

Here is the question that motivates everything. You want to know what happens to OLS when the zero conditional mean condition fails. Could you just take real data and check? Open the answer and read it, then think about why it is so damning. With real data you never see the error term, so you cannot tell whether it is correlated with x. And you never see the true beta, so even if you got an estimate you would have nothing to compare it against. Both halves of the comparison are invisible. That is precisely what simulation gives back to you.

Suppose you are interested in checking what happens to OLS estimators if E[u|x]=0 (the error term and x are not correlated) is violated.

Read the word in blue: you. You generate the data, and that single fact is what makes the whole method work. Two consequences, both on screen. First, you know the true parameter, because you typed it in. So when an estimate comes back you can measure how far off it is, which is impossible with real data. In the second example today we will break one condition on purpose and watch what happens, with nothing else moving.

Lecturer transcript

The q prefix reverses the question you have been asking. Pnorm took a value and returned a probability. Qnorm takes a probability and returns the value. Read the definition on screen: qnorm of x gives you the number q such that a draw falls below q with probability x. That is why the second bullet says it is the inverse of pnorm, and why running one inside the other gives you back what you started with. The default is again mean zero and standard deviation one, so unless you say otherwise you are getting quantiles of the standard Normal.

qnorm(x), where 0 < x < 1, gives you the number q such that a single random draw is less than q with probability x.

  • it is the inverse of pnorm(): pnorm(qnorm(x)) = x

We call the output of qnorm(x) the 100x\% quantile of the standard Normal distribution (because the default is mean = 0 and sd = 1 for qnorm()).

Lecturer transcript

Here is the quantile you will meet most often in this course. Qnorm of zero point nine five is one point six four, and the figure shows what that means: shade ninety five percent of the area starting from the left, and the point where you stop is one point six four, marked on the axis. Remember this number. When we get to hypothesis testing, one point six four and its two-tailed cousin one point nine six are the critical values that decide whether you reject. They are not magic constants; they are just quantiles of the Normal distribution, computed exactly like this.

Lecturer transcript

Monte Carlo Simulation: In Practice

Monte Carlo Simulation: Steps

Lecturer transcript

Here is the recipe, and every simulation in this course follows it. Specify the data generating process. Generate data from it. Get an estimate. Repeat many times. Compare your estimates against the true parameter you chose in step one. Now the question at the bottom, which is the important one. Why repeat? Because a single estimate from a single sample tells you nothing. Your estimator is a random variable, and one draw from a random variable cannot reveal its distribution. Repeating thousands of times is how you get to see the whole sampling distribution instead of one point from it.

  • specify the data generating process
  • generate data based on the data generating process
  • get an estimate based on the generated data (e.g. OLS, mean)
  • repeat the above steps many many times
  • compare your estimates with the true parameter

Lecturer transcript

Now the question we actually came for. What happens to beta-one-hat when the zero conditional mean condition fails? Note the hat carefully. The true beta-one does not change; it is a fixed feature of the world. What changes is the estimator, the thing we compute from data. This is the question we could not answer with real data, because we would need to see the error term to know the condition was violated, and see the true beta to know the estimate was wrong. In simulation we control both, so we can break the condition deliberately and watch the damage.

Question

What happens to \hat{\beta}_1 if E[u|x]\ne 0 when estimating y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x + u?

Lecturer transcript

Read lines two through four of the loop body, because that is where the violation is manufactured. We draw a variable called mu, and then mu goes into x, and the same mu also goes into u. So x and the error term share a common component by construction, which means they are correlated, which means the zero conditional mean condition fails. Everything else is untouched: the true intercept is one and the true slope is one, and we run ordinary OLS exactly as before. One thing broken, everything else fixed. That is the controlled experiment we promised at the start.

Lecturer transcript

Before running anything, let’s predict the answer, because a simulation you cannot predict is a simulation you cannot learn from. Both x and u contain mu, so the covariance between them is the variance of mu, which is one. And the variance of x is the variance of mu plus one, so two. The bias formula on screen turns those two numbers into a prediction: the estimates should center on one plus one half, which is one point five, against a true value of one. Write that number down. The next tab is the test of whether we understood our own model.

Both x and u contain \mu, so they are correlated by construction:

  • Cov(x, u) = Var(\mu) = 1
  • Var(x) = Var(\mu) + 1 = 2


And the bias of \hat{\beta}_1 is governed by exactly that ratio:

\hat{\beta}_1 \rightarrow \beta_1 + \frac{Cov(x, u)}{Var(x)} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} = 1.5

  • so the histogram should be centered at 1.5, not at the true value of 1
Lecturer transcript

And there it is. The estimates pile up around the red line at one point five, exactly as predicted, and the blue line at the true value of one sits well outside the bulk of the distribution. Compare this against the same picture in lecture 01-3, where the condition held and the histogram was centered on the truth. Now read the last bullet, which is the one to take home. More data would not save you. A larger sample tightens this histogram around one point five, so all it buys you is more and more confidence in the wrong number.

  • the estimates pile up around the red line, and the true value in blue sits well outside the bulk of the distribution

Lecturer transcript

This example is optional, but it answers a question you should be asking. We have checked that beta-hat is unbiased. But every regression table also reports a standard error, which comes from an estimated variance. Is that estimate any good? On screen, the true variance of beta-one-hat, sigma squared over the sum of squared deviations of x, and underneath it the estimator we actually compute, which replaces the unknown sigma squared with the average squared residual. The question at the bottom is whether that substitution is honest. Same method as before: build a world where we know the truth, and check.

Model

\begin{aligned} y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u \\ \end{aligned}
  • x\sim N(0,1)
  • u\sim N(0,1)
  • E[u|x]=0


Variance of the OLS estimator

True Variance of \hat{\beta}_1: V(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar{x})^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_X}

Its estimator: \widehat{V(\hat{\beta}_1)} =\frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{SST_X} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i^2}{n-2} \times \frac{1}{SST_X}


Question

Does the estimator really work? (Is it unbiased?)

Lecturer transcript

One design choice here is worth pausing on. The x values are drawn once, before the loop, so they are identical in every iteration. Only the error term is redrawn. That is deliberate: the variance formula on the previous tab conditions on x, so to check it we must hold x fixed and let only u vary. Inside the loop we store two things per iteration, the coefficient and the estimated variance from vcov. Ten thousand iterations this time rather than a thousand, because we are estimating the center of a distribution that is quite skewed.

Lecturer transcript

Here is the comparison, and the true variance is computed for you at the top. Sigma squared is four, because the error was two times a standard Normal, and the sum of squared deviations of x is the number shown, so the true variance is that small number underneath. Now run the cell. It averages your ten thousand estimated variances. Compare the two numbers. They agree, which means the variance estimator is unbiased too. That matters more than it might seem: every standard error, every t-statistic, and every confidence interval you ever report rests on this being true.

True Variance

  • SST_X = 112.07
  • \sigma^2 = 4

V(\hat{\beta}) = 4/112.07 = 0.0357


Check

Your Estimates of Variance of \hat{\beta}_1?

Lecturer transcript

One last look, because averages hide things. This plots the whole distribution of your ten thousand variance estimates, with a vertical line at the true value. Notice the shape. It is not symmetric; it has a long right tail, so individual estimates overshoot badly more often than the average suggests. The average still lands on the line, which is what unbiasedness promises. But look at how wide the spread is, and remember that in real work you get exactly one draw from this distribution. Unbiased does not mean accurate in any particular sample. It never has.

An exercise to run yourself, and it isolates something we have mentioned but not shown. How does variation in x affect OLS? Two models on screen, identical except for the independent variable. X-one is standard Normal, x-two has variance nine, so three times the standard deviation. Using MC simulations, find out how the variation in x affects the OLS estimators


Model setup

\begin{align} y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + u \\ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_2 + u \end{align}
  • x_1\sim N(0,1) and x_2\sim N(0,9) (so x_2 has three times the standard deviation of x_1)
  • u\sim N(0,1)
  • E[u|x_1]=0 and E[u|x_2]=0
Lecturer transcript

The code runs both regressions inside one loop on the same simulated data, storing two coefficients per iteration in a matrix rather than a vector. Run it and read the four numbers at the bottom carefully. The two means are both close to one, confirming both estimators are unbiased, as they must be. Now compare the two standard deviations. The one for x-two is roughly a third of the one for x-one. That is the variance formula from lecture 01-3 in action: more variation in x means a bigger denominator, which means a smaller variance.

Lecturer transcript

And here it is as a picture, which is worth more than the four numbers. Two densities, both centered on the true value of one, so unbiasedness is not what separates them. But the distribution for equation two, with the more spread out x, is dramatically narrower. Same truth, same sample size, same conditions satisfied, and yet one estimator is far more precise. That is the practical lesson: variation in your independent variable is information. An x that barely moves gives OLS little information per observation, although increasing the sample size can still improve precision.
