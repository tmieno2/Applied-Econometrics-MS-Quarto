04: Omitted Variable Bias and Multicollinearity

What variables to include or not

Transcript

Two questions in red on this slide, and they are the two you will face on every project you ever run. The first is a decision: you have a variable available, should you put it in the model? The second is a consequence: there is a variable you know matters and you simply cannot measure it, so what does that do to you? Notice these are not the same question. One is about a choice, the other about a constraint. The two concepts underneath, multicollinearity and omitted variable bias, are the answers, and this whole lecture is about telling them apart.

You often

  • face the decision of whether you should be including a particular variable or not: how do you make a right decision?

  • miss a variable that you know is important because it is not simply available: what are the consequences?

Two important concepts you need to be aware of:

  • Multicollinearity
  • Omitted Variable Bias

Multicollinearity and Omitted Variable Bias

  • Definition

Transcript

Two definitions to have in front of you, and the contrast matters. Multicollinearity is about relationships among your independent variables: two or more of them are highly correlated with each other. Note that the definition says nothing about y at all. Omitted variable bias is about something missing: an important variable is not in the model. Read the word in blue in each box, because those are the words we will be pressing on. What counts as a consequence of multicollinearity, and what makes an omitted variable important, are precisely what the four cases work out.

Definition: Multicollinearity

A phenomenon where two or more variables are highly correlated (negatively or positively) with each other ( consequences? )


Definition: Omitted Variable Bias

Bias caused by not including (omitting) important variables in the model

Transcript

The simplest possible setting in which the question can even be asked: y depends on two variables, x-one and x-two, plus an error. Your interest is entirely in x-one. That is the coefficient in your paper, the one your policy conclusion rests on. X-two is the variable you are deciding about. So read the objective box carefully. We are not asking whether x-two is estimated well. We are asking what happens to beta-one-hat depending on whether x-two is in the model or out of it. Two variables is enough to see everything; more would only add algebra.

Consider the following model,

y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,i} + \beta_2 x_{2,i} + u_i

Your interest is in estimating the impact of x_1 on y.

Objective

Using this simple model, we investigate what happens to the coefficient estimate on x_1 if you include/omit x_2.

Transcript

Four cases, and look at how they are constructed, because the structure is the whole design of the lecture. Two things can be true or false. Either beta-two is zero or it is not, meaning x-two either matters for y or it does not. And either x-two is correlated with x-one or it is not. Two binary conditions give four combinations, and those are exactly the four cases listed. Nothing else varies. So when you see a difference in the results, you know precisely which of the two conditions caused it, because only one thing changed.

The model: y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,i} + \beta_2 x_{2,i} + u_i

Case 1:

What happens if \beta_2=0, but include x_2 that is not correlated with x_1?

Case 2:

What happens if \beta_2=0, but include x_2 that is highly correlated with x_1?

Case 3:

What happens if \beta_2\ne 0, but omit x_2 that is not correlated with x_1?

Case 4:

What happens if \beta_2\ne 0, but omit x_2 that is highly correlated with x_1?

Transcript

Two things we track in every case: is beta-one-hat unbiased, and how large is its variance. And then the formula, which you already have from lecture 02. Read the three bullets under it, because they are the map for the entire lecture. Omitting a relevant variable pushes that variable into the error term, which changes the numerator. Including a correlated variable raises R-squared-j, which shrinks the denominator. The four cases are just the four combinations of those two effects being present or absent. Everything that follows is bookkeeping on this one expression.

  • Is \widehat{\beta}_1 unbiased, that is E[\widehat{\beta}_1]=\beta_1?

  • Var(\widehat{\beta}_1)? (how accurate the estimation of \widehat{\beta}_1 is)


Everything in this lecture comes out of one formula you already have, from lecture 02:

Var(\widehat{\beta}_j)= \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_j(1-R^2_j)}

  • omitting a relevant variable changes the numerator, because that variable goes into the error term
  • including a correlated variable changes the denominator, through R^2_j
  • the four cases below are just the four combinations of those two things happening or not

Case one, and it is the easy corner. Read the three conditions. X-one and x-two are uncorrelated, beta-two is zero, and the error is well behaved. So x-two is doing nothing at all: it does not affect y, and it carries no information about x-one either. The example makes that concrete. A farmer’s height genuinely has no effect on corn yield, and taller farmers do not apply more nitrogen. Ask yourself before we run anything: if you throw a variable like that into your regression, what could possibly go wrong? Case one is the sanity check.

True Model

y_i=\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,i} + \beta_2 x_{2,i} + u_i

  • cor(x_1,x_2) = 0
  • \beta_2=0
  • E[u_i|x_{1,i},x_{2,i}]=0

Example

\text{corn yield} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times N + \beta_2 \times \text{farmer's height} + u

  • a farmer’s height does not affect yield (\beta_2 = 0), and it is not related to how much nitrogen they apply (cor(x_1,x_2)=0)

Now variance, and we walk the formula term by term. The two questions below are the two moving parts. R-squared-j is the R-squared from regressing x-one on all the other covariates, and in this model there are no other covariates, so it is zero and the denominator is untouched. And the error variance: v is beta-two times x-two plus u, but beta-two is zero, so v is just u, and its variance is sigma-u squared. Both parts unchanged. That is why we get the answer we get on the comparison tab.

Here is the population comparison laid out. When x-two is orthogonal to x-one, the multicollinearity term is zero, and both models have error variance sigma-u squared. In an actual finite sample, their sample correlation will generally be small rather than exactly zero, and the larger model also estimates one more coefficient. Thus the variances need not be exactly identical, although they are nearly so in this design. The practical lesson is that an irrelevant, nearly uncorrelated control offers no precision gain and usually has only a small precision cost.

Transcript

The two takeaways from case one, and both are permission rather than warning. Including a variable that has no explanatory power and is uncorrelated with x-one leaves your variance exactly where it was. And omitting such a variable leaves you unbiased. So in this corner of the world it genuinely does not matter what you do. Remember that this is the only case where that is true. Three more cases follow, and each one breaks exactly one of the two conditions we imposed here, so you will be able to attribute every difference to the thing that changed.

  • If you include an irrelevant variable that has no explanatory power beyond x_1 and is not correlated with x_1 (EE_2), then the variance of the OLS estimator on x_1 will be the same as when you do not include x_2 as a covariate (EE_1)

  • If you omit an irrelevant variable that has no explanatory power beyond x_1 (EE_1) and is not correlated with x_1, then the OLS estimator on x_1 is still unbiased

Case two, and exactly one thing has changed from case one: x-one and x-two are now correlated. Beta-two is still zero, so x-two is still genuinely irrelevant to y. Read the example, because it is the shape this takes in practice. Fertilizer expenditure is almost entirely determined by how much nitrogen you applied, so the two are tightly related. But once you already know the nitrogen rate, the dollar figure tells you nothing further about yield. An irrelevant variable that happens to move with the one you care about. This is what multicollinearity means.

Now the algebra behind what case four showed us. Notice the two different symbols: beta-one-tilde for the estimator from the model that omits x-two, beta-one-hat for the one that includes it. Then the auxiliary regression, and read the direction carefully because this is where people go wrong. You regress the omitted variable on the included one, not the reverse. Delta-one is that slope, the covariance over the variance of x-one. The boxed result says the bias is beta-two times delta-one, and case four’s number came from exactly this.

True model

y_i=\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,i} + \beta_2 x_{2,i} + u_i


EE_1

y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,i} + v_{i} \;\; (\beta_2 x_{2,i} + u_{i})

Let \tilde{\beta}_1 denote the estimator of \beta_1 from this model


EE_2

y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,i} + \beta_2 x_{2,i} + u_{i}

Let \widehat{\beta}_1 and \widehat{\beta}_2 denote the estimator of \beta_1 and \beta_2


Relationship between x_1 and x_2

Regress the omitted variable on the included one:

x_{2,i} = \delta_0 + \delta_1 x_{1,i} + e_{i}, so that \delta_1 = \frac{Cov(x_1,x_2)}{Var(x_1)}


Important

Then, E[\tilde{\beta}_1] = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \cdot \delta_1, where \beta_2 \cdot \delta_1 is the bias.

That is, if you omit x_2 and regress y only on x_1, then the bias is the product of the impact of x_2 on y (\beta_2) and the slope you would get from regressing x_2 on x_1 (\delta_1).

  • note the direction of that auxiliary regression: omitted on included, not the other way around
Transcript

The four sign combinations, and they follow directly from the formula. Bias is beta-two times delta-one, and delta-one has the same sign as the correlation, so the sign of the bias is just the product of two signs. Work through the first line to convince yourself and the rest will follow. Then read the magnitude bullets and the note. The bias grows with the correlation and with the size of beta-two. It does not depend on beta-one at all. The coefficient you care about could be huge or zero; the bias is the same either way.

Direction of bias

  • Cor(x_1, x_2) > 0 and \beta_2 >0, then bias > 0
  • Cor(x_1, x_2) > 0 and \beta_2 <0, then bias < 0
  • Cor(x_1, x_2) < 0 and \beta_2 >0, then bias < 0
  • Cor(x_1, x_2) < 0 and \beta_2 <0, then bias > 0


Magnitude of bias

  • Holding the standard deviations of x_1 and x_2 fixed, the greater |Cor(x_1,x_2)| is, the greater the absolute bias

  • The greater |\beta_2| is, the greater the bias

Note

\beta_1 — the coefficient you actually care about — plays no role in the bias. Omitting x_2 shifts \widehat{\beta}_1 by \beta_2\cdot\delta_1 regardless of how large or small the true effect of x_1 is.

  • Distance to city center has a negative impact on house price (\gamma < 0)

One from my own field. You are estimating the effect of precipitation on groundwater use, and irrigation technology is omitted. Farmers in drier places adopt center pivot more, so precipitation is negatively correlated with center pivot adoption. And center pivot applies water more efficiently, so gamma is negative. Negative times negative gives a positive bias. Now notice something useful: you would expect the true effect of precipitation on groundwater use to be negative, and the bias pushes upward, against your expectation. The next tab is about why that is good news.

\begin{aligned} \text{groundwater use} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{precipitation} + (\gamma \cdot \text{center pivot} + \mu) \end{aligned}

\text{groundwater use}: groundwater use by a farmer for irrigated production

\text{center pivot}: 1 if center pivot is used, 0 if flood irrigation (less effective) is used

  • Farmers who have relatively low precipitation during the growing season tend to adopt center pivot more
  • center pivot applied water more efficiently than flood irrigation (\gamma < 0)

What is the direction of bias on \hat{\beta}?

When the direction of the bias is the opposite of the expected coefficient on the variable of interest, you can claim that even after suffering from the bias, you are still seeing the impact of the variable interest. So, it is strong evidence that you would have had an even stronger estimated impact.

  • Example 1
  • Example 2
Transcript

Here is why signing the bias is worth the effort. The true effect is minus ten, the bias is plus five, so what you actually estimate is minus five. You never see the first two numbers, only the last. But if you can argue the bias is positive, then your estimate of minus five is a conservative one: the truth must be even more negative. So you can say, with a straight face, that the effect is at least this large. The bias worked against you and the result survived anyway.

\begin{aligned} \text{groundwater use} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{precipitation} + (\gamma \cdot \text{center pivot} + \mu) \end{aligned}

  • The true \beta is -10 ( you do not observe this )
  • The bias on \widehat{\beta} is 5 ( you do not observe this )
  • \widehat{\beta} is -5 ( you only observe this )

You believe the direction of bias is positive (you need provide reasoning behind your belief), and yet, the estimated coefficient is still negative. So, you can be quite confident that the sign of the impact of precipitation is negative. You can say your estimate is a conservative estimate of the impact of precipitation on groundwater use.

Transcript

And here is the case where the same reasoning gives you nothing. True effect minus ten, bias minus five, estimate minus fifteen. Again you see only the last number. But now the bias points the same direction as your finding, so it could be doing all the work. A large enough negative bias could produce a negative estimate even if the truth were positive. Read the last sentence: there is nothing you can do. The direction of the bias determines whether this argument is available to you, and you do not get to choose it.

\begin{aligned} \text{house price} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{dist to incinerators} + (\gamma \cdot \text{dist to city center} + \mu) \end{aligned}

  • The true \beta is -10 ( you do not observe this )
  • The bias on \widehat{\beta} is -5 ( you do not observe this )
  • \widehat{\beta} is -15 ( you only observe this )

You believe the direction of bias is negative, and the estimated coefficient is negative. So, unlike the case above, you cannot be confident that \widehat{\beta} would have been negative if it were not for the bias (by observing dist to city center and include it as a covariate). It is very much possible that the degree of bias is so large that the estimated coefficient turns negative even though the true sign of \beta is positive. In this case, there is nothing you can do.
