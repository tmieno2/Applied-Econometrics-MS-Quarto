Lecturer transcript

Five steps, and every test in this lecture is one of them wearing different clothes, so it is worth reading them now and coming back at the end. Notice how much work step two is doing. You need the distribution of your test statistic assuming the null is true, and if you cannot get that, you have no test. Steps four and five are where judgement enters: you choose how much error you will tolerate, and then check how unlikely your number was. Everything else is mechanical. Read the red phrases twice, because “if the null is true” is the assumption the whole logic hangs on.

Here is the general step of any hypothesis testing:

  • Step 1: specify the null (H_0) and alternative (H_1) hypotheses

  • Step 2: find the distribution of the test statistic if the null hypothesis is true

  • Step 3: calculate the test statistic based on the data and regression results

  • Step 4: define the significance level

  • Step 5: check how unlikely that you get the actual test statistic (found at Step 3) if indeed the null hypothesis is true

A deliberately artificial example, and the artificiality is the point. We want to test whether the expected value of x is one. Read the state of knowledge: we somehow already know the variance is four. That never happens in practice, and we assume it here only so the first pass through the logic has nothing hidden in it. Your estimator is the sample mean, which you already know is unbiased from lecture 03. So we have an estimator and a target value, and the question is how to decide whether the two are far enough apart to matter.

Goal

Suppose you want to test if the expected value of a normally distributed random variable (x) is 1 or not.


State of Knowledge

We do know x follows a normal distribution and its variance is 4 for some reason.


Your Estimator

Your estimator is the sample mean: \theta = \sum_{i=1}^J x_i/J

Lecturer transcript

Two math asides and then the payoff, so follow the chain. The first says scaling a random variable scales its variance by the square of the factor. The second says shifting a Normal moves its mean but leaves its variance alone. Put them together and any Normal can be turned into a standard Normal: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. Now apply that to the sample mean. Its variance is four over J, so its standard deviation is two over root J. Dividing by that gives root J over two, times theta minus alpha. That is the last line.

Math Aside 1

Var(ax) = a^2 Var(X)

So, we know that \theta \sim N(\alpha, 4/J) (of course \alpha is not known).


Math Aside 2

If x \sim N(a, b), then, x-a \sim N(0, b) (shift)

So, \frac{x-a}{\sqrt{b}} \sim N(0, 1) (combined with Math Aside 1)


This means,

Since \theta = \sum_{i=1}^J x_i/J and x_i \sim N(\alpha, 4),

  • Var(\theta) = J \times \frac{1}{J^2}Var(x) = 4/J
  • \frac{\sqrt{J}}{2} \cdot (\theta - \alpha)\sim N(0, 1).
Lecturer transcript

Here is where the null hypothesis actually enters. Up to now alpha was unknown, so the expression on the left was not something you could compute. But if the null says alpha equals one, substitute it in, and suddenly every symbol is a number you have. Read the sentence carefully and notice the red phrase: you subtract the value under the null first, then multiply by root J, then divide by two. Students routinely drop the subtraction. The picture on the right is what that quantity follows if the null is true. It is just the standard Normal.

We established that \frac{\sqrt{J}}{2} \cdot (\theta - \alpha)\sim N(0, 1).

The null hypothesis is \alpha = 1.

If \alpha = 1 is indeed true, then \sqrt{J} \times (\theta - 1)/2 \sim N(0, 1).

In other words, if you take the sample mean, subtract the value under the null, multiply by the square root of the number of observations, and divide by 2, the result follows the standard Normal distribution like below.

Lecturer transcript

Now put numbers in. A hundred observations, sample mean of two, null value of one. Root of a hundred is ten, times the gap of one, divided by two, gives five. Now look at the figure and find five on the horizontal axis. It is not there; the curve has effectively no mass out that far. So either the null is true and something absurdly unlikely happened, or the null is false. That is the entire logic of hypothesis testing, and you have just done one. What we have not yet done is say how unlikely counts as unlikely enough.

How unlikely is it to get the number you got (5) if the null hypothesis is indeed true?

Lecturer transcript

A stocktake before we can go further. We have unbiasedness from MLR one through four, and a variance formula from MLR five. But read the line in blue: we have never said anything about the shape of the error distribution. Unbiasedness and variance are statements about the first two moments only. To do a test you need the whole distribution of your statistic, not two summary numbers, so we need more. The parenthesis at the end is a promise worth remembering: this assumption turns out to be avoidable in large samples, which is the asymptotics lecture.

So far, we learned that:

  • OLS estimators are unbiased under MLR.1 ~ MLR.4
  • Variance of the OLS estimator of \beta_j is \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_x\cdot (1-R^2_j)} under MLR.1 ~ MLR.5

We have NOT made any assumptions about the distribution of the error term!!

In order to perform hypothesis testing, we need to make assumptions about the distribution of error term (this is not strictly true, but more on this later)

Lecturer transcript

This is MLR six, and read it carefully because it is much stronger than it looks. The headline is that u is Normally distributed. But look at the other red word: independent. Independence of the error from the regressors is far more than zero correlation. Read the second callout, which spells out the consequences. Independence gives you a zero conditional mean, so MLR four comes free. It also gives you constant conditional variance, so MLR five comes free too. We are assuming a great deal here, which is why the asymptotics lecture is such a relief.

For the purpose of hypothesis testing, we will make the following assumption:

Normality of the error term

The population error u is independent of the explanatory variables x_1,\dots,x_k and is normally distributed with zero mean and variance \sigma^2:

u\sim N(0,\sigma^2)

Note

The normality assumption is much more than error term being distributed as Normal.

Independence of the error term implies

  • E[u|x] = 0
  • Var[u|x]= \sigma^2

So, we are necessarily assuming MLR.4 and MLR.5 hold by the independence assumption.

Lecturer transcript

Two consequences, one per column. On the left, y conditional on x is Normal, with the regression line as its mean. That follows immediately: y is a constant plus a Normal error. On the right is the one we need. Because beta-j-hat is a weighted sum of the y values, and sums of Normals are Normal, the estimator is itself Normally distributed, centred on the true beta. Subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation, and you have a standard Normal. Now read the question at the bottom, because the answer is no, and the next tab says why.

distribution of the dependent variable

The distribution of y conditional on x is a Normal distribution

y|x \sim N(\beta_0+\beta_1 x_1+\dots+\beta_k x_k,\sigma^2)

  • E[y|x] is \beta_0+\beta_1 x_1+\dots+\beta_k x_k
  • u|x is N(0,\sigma^2)

distribution of the OLS estimator

If the MLR.1 through MLR.6 are satisfied, OLS estimators are also Normally distributed!

\widehat{\beta}_j \sim N(\beta_j,Var(\widehat{\beta}_j))

which means,

\frac{\widehat{\beta}_j-\beta_j}{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)} \sim N(0,1)

Question

Okay, so are we going to use this for testing involving \beta_j?

Lecturer transcript

And here is the catch the question was pointing at. The expression on the previous tab used the true standard error, which contains sigma squared, which you do not know. Replace it with the estimated standard error and the distribution is no longer Normal. It becomes t with n minus k minus one degrees of freedom. The extra spread in the t distribution is the cost of estimating the denominator: you have two random things in the ratio now instead of one. Note the corrected formula in the parenthesis, and the square root in particular, because the standard error is the root of the variance.

In practice, we need to estimate se(\beta_j). If we use \widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)} instead of se(\widehat{\beta}_j), then,

\frac{\widehat{\beta}_j-\beta_j}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}} \sim t_{n-k-1}

where n-k-1 is the degree of freedom of residual.

(Note: \widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)} = \sqrt{\widehat{\sigma}^2/\big[SST_X\cdot (1-R_j^2)\big]} — the standard error is the square root of the estimated variance)

Lecturer transcript

This is a reference tab rather than something to work through, and I would bookmark it. The distinction that matters most is the hat. Sigma squared is the true error variance; sigma-hat squared is what you compute from the residuals. Var of beta-j-hat is a true quantity; the version with a hat over the whole thing is your estimate of it. And se is the square root of a variance, never the variance itself. Almost every algebra mistake in this lecture comes from losing track of which of these you are holding.

  • k: the number of explanatory variables included except the intercept
  • \sigma^2: the true variance of the error term
  • \widehat{\sigma^2}: the estimator (estimate) of the variance of the error term
    • \widehat{\sigma^2} = \frac{\sum \widehat{u}_i^2}{n-k-1}
  • SST_X = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2
  • \widehat{\beta}_j: OLS estimator (estimate) on explanatory variable x_j
    • \widehat{\beta} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{SST_X} (for simple univariate regression)
  • var(\widehat{\beta}_j): the true variance of \widehat{\beta}_j
  • R^2_j: R^2 when you regress x_j on all the other covariates (mathematical expression omitted)
  • \widehat{var(\widehat{\beta}_j)}: Estimator (estimate) of var(\widehat{\beta}_j)
    • \widehat{var(\widehat{\beta}_j)} = \frac{\widehat{\sigma^2}}{SST_X\cdot(1-R^2_j)}
  • se(\widehat{\beta}_j): square root of var(\widehat{\beta}_j)
  • \widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}: square root of \widehat{var(\widehat{\beta}_j)}

Lecturer transcript

The courtroom analogy, and it repays taking seriously. You are the attorney. The null hypothesis is what you want to knock down: the defendant is not guilty. The alternative is what you are arguing for. Notice the asymmetry this builds in. You do not prove the null true; you either reject it or fail to reject it, exactly as a court either convicts or fails to convict. And the burden of proof sits on the alternative. That is why we will say “fail to reject” rather than “accept”, and why an insignificant result is not evidence of no effect.

Statistical hypothesis testing involves two hypotheses: Null and Alternative hypotheses.

Pretend that you are an attorney who indicted a defendant who you think committed a crime.


Null Hypothesis

Hypothesis that you would like to reject (defendant is not guilty)


Alternative Hypothesis

Hypothesis you are in support of (defendant is guilty)

Lecturer transcript

Two shapes of alternative, and which you use changes where you look. With a one-sided alternative you have a directional claim, so you only care about one tail of the distribution. With a two-sided alternative you care about departures in either direction, so you look at both tails. The practical consequence is that for a given significance level the two-sided critical value is further out, because you have to split your error budget across two tails. Then read the callout: these critical values are just quantiles, the same computation as qnorm in lecture 03.

one-sided alternative

H_0: \beta_j = 0 H_1: \beta_j > 0

You look at the positive end of the t-distribution to see if the t-statistic you obtained is more extreme than the level of error you accept (significance level).


two-sided alternative

H_0: \beta_j = 0 H_1: \beta_j \ne 0

You look at both ends of the t-distribution to see if the t-statistic you obtained is more extreme than the level of error you accept (significance level).


This is where lecture 03 pays off

The numbers we are about to use as critical values are just quantiles, computed exactly the way we computed qnorm(0.95) = 1.64 in the Monte Carlo lecture. The only change is which distribution you ask: qt() instead of qnorm(), because we had to estimate \sigma rather than know it.

  • One-sided: 1% significance

Lecturer transcript

The significance level, and then the thing this slide previously left out. The definition is the probability of rejecting a true null: how often you convict an innocent defendant. Now read the table. There are two ways to be wrong, and lowering your significance level trades one for the other. Push from five percent to one percent and you convict fewer innocents, but you also acquit more of the guilty, which is Type two error. One minus that is the power of your test. The last bullet is the practical payoff: not significant does not mean no effect.

Definition

The probability of rejecting the null when the null is actually true (the probability that you wrongly claim that the null hypothesis is wrong even though it is true in reality: Type I error)

A lower significance level requires stronger evidence before rejecting the null and reduces the long-run probability of a Type I error. It does not tell us the probability that the null hypothesis is true.


But lowering it is not free

There are two ways to be wrong, and they trade off against each other:

H_0 is true H_0 is false
You reject H_0 Type I error (probability = significance level) correct
You do not reject H_0 correct Type II error
  • the significance level is a choice about how much Type I error you will tolerate
  • pushing it down from 5% to 1% moves the critical value further out, so you reject less often — which means more Type II error: real effects you fail to detect
  • 1 - P(\text{Type II error}) is called the power of the test: the chance of detecting an effect that is genuinely there
  • this is why “not statistically significant” does not mean “no effect”. It may just mean your test had too little power to see it

Lecturer transcript

The p-value inverts the question. Instead of fixing a level and asking whether you reject, it asks: at what level would this result have been on the boundary? The figure shows a statistic of two point one six, and the shaded area either side of it is three point one percent. So if you had set your level at three point one percent, this result would sit exactly at the critical value. Any level above that and you reject; any level below and you do not. Read the decision rule at the bottom, and note it always agrees with the critical value approach.

Definition

The smallest significance level at which the null hypothesis would be rejected (the probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as we did if the null hypothesis is true)

  • Suppose the t-statistic you got is 2.16. If the null hypothesis is true, the probability of observing a test statistic at least this extreme is 3.1%.

  • Thus, 3.1% is the smallest significance level at which the null would be rejected, which is the definition of the p-value.

Decision rule

If the p-value is lower than your choice of significance level, then you reject the null.

This decision rule of course results in the same test results as the one we saw that uses a t-value and critical value.

Read both halves of this slide, because the second is the one people get wrong. A ninety-five percent confidence interval means that if you repeated the whole exercise on many samples, ninety-five percent of the intervals you computed would contain the true parameter. The property belongs to the procedure, not to any one interval. What it is not, on the lower half, is a probability statement about the interval you actually have. What confidence interval is not

The probability that a realized CI calculated from specific sample data includes the true parameter

Lecturer transcript

The general recipe, and it is short. To get an A percent interval, chop off the same amount of probability from each tail: A percent in the middle means one hundred minus A percent left over, split in two. So the lower bound is the hundred minus A over two quantile and the upper bound is its mirror image. Note the parentheses carefully. It is one hundred minus A, then divided by two, not one hundred minus A over two. That distinction is the difference between two point five percent and fifty-two point five percent.

For the assumed distribution of statistic x, the A\% confidence interval of x is the range with

  • lower bound: (100 - A)/2 percent quantile of x
  • upper bound: 100-(100 - A)/2 percent quantile of x
Lecturer transcript

The recipe with numbers. For a ninety-five percent interval, five percent is left over, split into two and a half at each end, so you want the two point five and ninety-seven point five percent quantiles. If your statistic is standard Normal those quantiles are minus one point nine six and one point nine six, and the figure shows exactly that: the red tails together hold five percent of the area. Recognise the number. It is the same one point nine six that was the two-sided critical value two slides ago, which is not a coincidence.

For the 95% CI (A = 95),

  • lower bound: 2.5 \big((100-95)/2\big) percent quantile of x
  • upper bound: 97.5 \big(100-(100-95)/2\big) percent quantile of x

If x follows the standard normal distribution (x \sim N(0, 1)), then,the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles are -1.96 and 1.96, respectively. So, the 95% CI of x is [-1.96, 1.96].

Lecturer transcript

The derivation, and it has two moves. We know the distribution of the standardised quantity, so we know its interval directly: the quantiles of the t distribution. But you want an interval for beta-j, not for that ratio, so you have to invert. Solve the ratio for beta-j and you get beta-hat minus t times the standard error. Then read the last two bullets: scale each quantile by the standard error and shift by the estimate. The lower quantile is negative and the upper positive, which is what puts the estimate in the middle.

Under the assumption of MLR.1 through MLR.6 (which includes the normality assumption of the error), we learned that

\frac{\widehat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}} \sim t_{n-k-1}

So, following the general procedure we discussed in the previous slide, the A% confidence interval of \frac{\widehat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}} is

  • lower bound: (100 − A)/2% quantile of the t_{n-k-1} distribution (let’s call this Q_l)
  • upper bound: 100 - (100 − A)/2% quantile of the t_{n-k-1} distribution (let’s call this Q_h)

But, we want the A% CI of \beta_j, not \frac{\widehat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}}. Solving for \beta_j,

\beta_j = \widehat{\beta}_j - t_{n-k-1}\times \widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}

So, to get the A% CI of \beta_j, we scale the CI of \frac{\widehat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}} by \widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)} and then shift by \widehat{\beta}_j. Because the t distribution is symmetric, the minus sign above just swaps which quantile lands on which end:

  • lower bound: Q_l \times \widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)} + \widehat{\beta}_j
  • upper bound: Q_h \times \widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)} + \widehat{\beta}_j

Note that Q_l is negative and Q_h is positive.

The fact that makes this work, in the box. For any linear combination of OLS coefficients, the standardised version follows a t distribution with the same degrees of freedom as before. \frac{\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\alpha})}} \sim t_{n-k-1}

The explanation, and then the connection back. The standard errors are large because the two variables are so alike that the estimation cannot tell their individual contributions apart. Read the callout, because this is lecture 04 seen from the other side. There we showed correlation inflates the variance through the one minus R-squared-j term. Lecturer transcript

One last connection, and it ties the two halves of this lecture together. Testing a linear combination is an F-test with one restriction. The exact finite-sample identity is that an F statistic with one numerator degree of freedom equals the corresponding t statistic squared. The Wald chi-squared statistic printed above has the same numerical value, although using a chi-squared rather than an F reference distribution is asymptotic. With this large sample the critical values are very close.

The test of a linear combination of the parameters we looked at earlier is a special case of F-test where the number of restriction is 1.

It can be shown that square root of F_{1, df} follows the t_{df} distribution.

So, we can actually use the F-test for this type of hypothesis testing because F_{1,n-k-1} \sim t_{n-k-1}^2.

Lecturer transcript

Everything in this lecture was one recipe, and this is it, unchanged from the first slide. State the hypotheses. Find the distribution of a statistic assuming the null. Compute it. Pick a level, which fixes a critical value. Compare. Read the sentence at the bottom, because it is the useful compression: the only thing that varied from test to test was which statistic you built and which distribution you looked it up in. If a future test looks unfamiliar, ask those two questions and it usually stops being unfamiliar.

Every test we did followed the same five steps from the very first slide:

  • write down H_0 and H_1
  • find the distribution of a test statistic assuming H_0 is true
  • compute the statistic from your data
  • pick a significance level, which fixes a critical value
  • reject if your statistic is more extreme than that critical value

Lecturer transcript

The four things we did, side by side. Read down the statistic column and notice how similar the first two are: an estimate, minus its value under the null, over its standard error. The only difference is what counts as the estimate. The third is genuinely different, because it compares two model fits rather than standardising a single number, and that is why it can answer a question the first two cannot. And read the two bullets: the covariance term is why the variance-covariance matrix had to appear at all.

Question Statistic Distribution under H_0 In R
Is one coefficient equal to a value? \dfrac{\widehat{\beta}_j - \beta_j^0}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\beta}_j)}} t_{n-k-1} broom::tidy(), or by hand
Is a linear combination equal to a value? \dfrac{\widehat{\alpha} - \alpha^0}{\widehat{se(\widehat{\alpha})}} t_{n-k-1} car::linearHypothesis()
Are q coefficients jointly zero? \dfrac{(SSR_r-SSR_u)/q}{SSR_u/(n-k-1)} F_{q,\,n-k-1} car::linearHypothesis()
What range of values is consistent with the data? the CI, not a test t_{n-k-1} broom::tidy(conf.int = TRUE)
  • the second row needs Cov(\widehat{\beta}_1,\widehat{\beta}_2), which is why the variance-covariance matrix appeared
  • the third row is the only one that cannot be built from a single coefficient and its standard error
Lecturer transcript

Two connections that reduce the count. First, the F-test with one restriction is the t-test squared, which we confirmed numerically two slides ago. Second, and this one is worth internalising: a confidence interval and a two-sided test are the same statement. The ninety-five percent interval is precisely the set of null values you would fail to reject at five percent. So reporting an interval reports infinitely many tests at once, which is a good reason to prefer it. Then read the callout, which is the thing I most want you to carry away.

Two connections worth remembering, because they mean there are fewer ideas here than there appear to be:

  • F with one restriction is just t squared. F_{1,\,n-k-1} \sim t_{n-k-1}^2, which is why car::linearHypothesis() gave the same answer as the t-test we did by hand.

  • The confidence interval and the two-sided test are the same statement. The 95% CI is exactly the set of null values you would fail to reject at the 5% level. If a CI excludes zero, the t-test rejects \beta_j = 0; if it contains zero, it does not.


The one thing to carry into your own work

An individually insignificant coefficient is not evidence of no effect. The MLB players and the simulation both showed variables that looked useless one at a time and mattered a great deal together. When variables are correlated, test them jointly.
