Question

  • Among all the possible values of \widehat{\beta}_0 and \widehat{\beta}_1, which one is the best?
  • What criteria do we use (what does the best even mean?)
Lecturer transcript

Here is the raw material for that criterion. Pick any values of beta-hat-zero and beta-hat-one, and that gives you a modeled value for every house: beta-hat-zero plus beta-hat-one times x-i. The residual is what is left over, the actual price minus the modeled price. Look at the figure, where I have taken twenty-five houses and drawn their residuals in red. Each red segment runs from a point to the line, so a long one means the line missed that house badly. Two things to keep straight. Residuals are computable, unlike the error term u, which you never see. And move the line, and every red segment changes.

For particular values of \widehat{\beta}_0 and \widehat{\beta}_1 you pick, the modeled value of y for individual i is \widehat{\beta}_0 + \widehat{\beta}_1 x_i.

Then, the residual for individual i is:

\widehat{u}_i = y_i - (\widehat{\beta}_0 + \widehat{\beta}_1 x_i)

That is, the residual is the observed value of the dependent variable less the modeled value. For different values of \widehat{\beta}_0 and \widehat{\beta}_1, you have a different value of residual.

The vertical red segments below are the residuals: each one runs from an observed point to the line.

Lecturer transcript

Now we can state the criterion, and it is the obvious one once you have seen those red segments: choose the line that makes them small overall. Specifically, square each residual, add them all up, and pick the beta-hats that make that sum as small as it can be. That is what ordinary least squares means, and the name is a literal description: least, squares. The mathematics below says exactly the same thing in symbols, minimize over beta-hat-zero and beta-hat-one the sum from i equals one to n of u-hat-i squared. That is the whole idea. Everything that follows is just carrying out the minimization.

Idea of OLS (Ordinary Least Squares)

Let’s find the values of \widehat{\beta}_0 and \widehat{\beta}_1 that minimize the sum of the squared residuals!


Mathematically

Solve the following minimization problem:

Min_{\widehat{\beta}_0,\widehat{\beta}_1} \sum_{i=1}^n \widehat{u}_i^2, \text{where} \;\; \widehat{u}_i=y_i-(\widehat{\beta}_0+\widehat{\beta}_1 x_i)

Lecturer transcript

Two questions on this slide, and I want you to think about them rather than take the squaring on faith. First, why square? Suppose you just added the raw residuals up. Points above the line contribute positive numbers, points below contribute negative ones, and they cancel, so a wildly wrong line could score zero. Squaring removes the sign, and it also punishes one enormous miss more than several small ones. Second, why not absolute values? You could, and that method exists; it is called least absolute deviations. Squares win mainly because they give you something you can differentiate and solve by hand, which is exactly what we do next.

Questions

  • Why do we square the residuals, and then sum them up together? What’s gonna happen if you just sum up residuals?

  • How about taking the absolute value of residuals, and then sum them up?

Lecturer transcript

Here is the calculus, and you should watch it once even if you never do it again. We are minimizing S, the sum of squared residuals, over two unknowns, so we take a partial derivative with respect to each and set both to zero. Now look at what the two first-order conditions say once you divide out the minus two. The sum of the residuals is zero, and the sum of x times the residuals is zero. Those are not assumptions we are making; they are consequences of the arithmetic, and they hold in every OLS regression you will ever run. Solve the pair and you get the formulas at the bottom.

Minimization problem to solve

Min_{\widehat{\beta}_0,\widehat{\beta}_1} \sum_{i=1}^n [y_i-(\widehat{\beta}_0+\widehat{\beta}_1 x_i)]^2

Steps

  • partial differentiation of the objective function with respect to \widehat{\beta}_0 and \widehat{\beta}_1
  • solve for \widehat{\beta}_0 and \widehat{\beta}_1

Min_{\widehat{\beta}_0,\widehat{\beta}_1} \sum_{i=1}^n [y_i-(\widehat{\beta}_0+\widehat{\beta}_1 x_i)]^2

Write the objective as S(\widehat{\beta}_0,\widehat{\beta}_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n [y_i-(\widehat{\beta}_0+\widehat{\beta}_1 x_i)]^2.

FOC

\def\sumn{\sum_{i=1}^{n}} \begin{align} \frac{\partial S}{\partial \widehat{\beta}_0}=& -2 \sumn [y_i-(\widehat{\beta}_0+\widehat{\beta}_1 x_i)] = -2\sumn \widehat{u}_i = 0 \\\\ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \widehat{\beta}_1}=& -2 \sumn x_i\cdot [y_i-(\widehat{\beta}_0+\widehat{\beta}_1 x_i)] = -2\sumn x_i\cdot \widehat{u}_i = 0 \end{align}

Dividing each by -2 leaves the two conditions OLS always satisfies:

\def\sumn{\sum_{i=1}^{n}} \sumn \widehat{u}_i = 0 \;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\; \sumn x_i \widehat{u}_i = 0

OLS estimators: analytical formula

\def\sumn{\sum_{i=1}^{n}} \begin{aligned} \widehat{\beta}_1 & = \frac{\sumn (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sumn (x_i-\bar{x})^2},\\\\ \widehat{\beta}_0 & = \bar{y}-\widehat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}, \\\\ \text{where} & \;\; \bar{y} = \sumn y_i/n \;\; \text{and} \;\;\bar{x} = \sumn x_i/n \end{aligned}

Lecturer transcript

This is a small distinction with a large payoff later, so let’s be careful with it. An estimator is a rule, a formula that says what to do with whatever data you are handed. An estimate is the number that comes out when you feed that rule your particular data. The formula on screen is the estimator; six point five nine nine is the estimate, for these five hundred and forty-six houses. And here is why it matters. Hand the same estimator a different sample of houses and you get a different number out. The estimate moves around. That variability is the entire subject of the next lecture.

Lecturer transcript

The last topic today is R-squared, and I want you to have the one-sentence version before any formulas arrive. R-squared measures how much better your model predicts the dependent variable than the laziest possible model does. And that lazy model is worth naming, because it is the benchmark the whole measure is built on: just guess the average price for every single house, ignoring lot size entirely. So R-squared answers one question. How much did knowing lot size actually buy me, over knowing nothing but the average? Everything in the next four tabs is machinery for turning that question into a number.

R^2 is a measure of how good your model is in predicting the dependent variable (explaining variations in the dependent variable) compared to just using the average of the dependent variable as the predictor.

Lecturer transcript

Before the formula, here is the decomposition it rests on, and it is only algebra. Any observed y-i splits into two pieces: the fitted value, y-hat-i, and the residual, u-hat-i. Now subtract the sample average, y-bar, from both sides, and read the three bullets. On the left, y-i minus y-bar, is how far this house’s price sits from the average price; that is the total deviation you are trying to account for. On the right, y-hat-i minus y-bar is the part your model explains, and u-hat-i is the part it does not. Every measure of fit compares those two right-hand pieces.

You can decompose observed value of y into two parts: fitted value and residual

y_i=\widehat{y}_i +\widehat{u}_i, \;\;\text{where}\;\; \widehat{y}_i = \widehat{\beta}_0+\widehat{\beta}_1 x_i

now, subtracting \bar{y} (sample average of y),

y_i-\bar{y}=\widehat{y}_i-\bar{y}+\widehat{u}_i

  • y_i-\bar{y}: how far away the actual value of y for ith observation from the sample average \bar{y} is (actual deviation from the mean)
  • \widehat{y_i}-\bar{y}: how far away the predicted value of y for ith observation from the sample average \bar{y} is (explained deviation from the mean)
  • \widehat{u_i}: the residual for ith observation
Lecturer transcript

Here is that split drawn for one real house, so the algebra has a picture attached to it. The red horizontal line is y-bar, the average price. The blue line is the fitted model. Now find the black dot over on the right: that is one house. The green arrow runs from the average up to the fitted line, and that is the explained part, what knowing this house’s lot size buys you. The purple arrow runs from the fitted line to where the house actually sold, and that is the residual, what the model missed. Green plus purple is the total deviation.

The three pieces, drawn for a single observation. The red line is \bar{y}; the blue line is the fitted model.

  • green + purple together make up y_i-\bar{y}, the actual deviation from the mean
  • green alone, \widehat{y}_i-\bar{y}, is the part the model explains
  • purple alone, \widehat{u}_i, is the part it does not
Lecturer transcript

Three sums, and the names are more confusing than the mathematics, so take them slowly. Total sum of squares, SST, adds up the squared total deviations: how much price varies overall. Explained sum of squares, SSE, adds up the squared explained parts, the green arrows. Residual sum of squares, SSR, adds up the squared residuals, the purple arrows, and you have met this one already, because it is exactly the quantity OLS was chosen to minimize. One warning: some textbooks swap the letters SSE and SSR completely, so read the definition rather than trusting the acronym.

total sum of squares (SST)

SST\equiv \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2

explained sum of squares (SSE) SSE\equiv \sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{y}_i-\bar{y})^2

residual sum of squares (SSR) SSR\equiv \sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{u}_i^2

Lecturer transcript

Now the definition. R-squared is one minus SSR over SST. Where that comes from is the three lines below: total equals explained plus residual, rearrange, divide through by SST. So R-squared is equally the explained share, SSE over SST, and it sits between zero and one as long as your model includes an intercept. And here is the number for our regression, computed just below: about zero point two nine. Lot size accounts for roughly twenty-nine percent of the variation in house price. The other seventy-one percent is everything in u, bedrooms, location, condition, the year it sold.

Definition

R^2 = 1 - SSR/SST


Where did it come from?

\begin{align} & SST = SSE + SSR \\ \Rightarrow & SSE = SST - SSR \\ \Rightarrow & SSE/SST = 1 - SSR/SST = R^2\\ \end{align}

The value of R^2 always lies between 0 and 1 as long as an intercept is included in the econometric model.


What does it measure?

R^2 is a measure of how much improvement in predicting the dependent variable you’ve made by including independent variable(s) (y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x+u) compared to when simply using the mean of dependent variable as the predictor (y=\beta_0+u).


For our model

That is how much of the variation in house price lot size alone accounts for. The rest is everything in u — bedrooms, location, condition, the year it sold.

Lecturer transcript

Now the warning, and it is the most important thing on this slide. R-squared tells you nothing about whether you have estimated beta-one well. Read the two blocks. We can always check how well a model predicts the dependent variable, because we observe the dependent variable. We can never check how well we estimated beta-one, because nobody ever shows us the true beta-one. So we are in the awkward position of being able to measure the thing we do not much care about, and unable to measure the thing we do. That is exactly why the next topic is assumptions.

Important

  • R^2 tells nothing about how well you have estimated the causal ceteris paribus impact of x on y (\beta_1).
  • As economists, we typically do not care how well we can predict the dependent variable; we care how well we have estimated \beta_1. (We predict outcomes; we estimate parameters.)

Problem

  • While we observe the dependent variable (otherwise you cannot run regression), we cannot observe \beta_1.
  • So, we get to check how good estimated models are in predicting the dependent variable (which we do not care about), but we can never test whether they have estimated \beta_1 well.
  • This means that we need to carefully examine whether the assumptions necessary for good estimation of \beta_1 are satisfied (next topic).
