Univariate Regression: Introduction

Population, Sample, and Econometrics

  • Population
  • Sample
Transcript

Everything in econometrics starts with a question about a population, so let’s be precise about that word. A population is the set of all the individuals, or farms, or firms you want to learn about. Every one of them, not the ones you happen to have data on. Look at the examples: if your question is about education and income in the United States, your population is every person in the country. Now read the Important block, because this is the part students skip. A population is not a fact about the world, it is a consequence of your question. Change the question from global to American and you have changed the population.

Definition

A set of ALL individuals, items, or phenomena that you are interested in learning about


Example

  • Suppose you are interested in the impact of education on income across the U.S. Then, the population is all the individuals in U.S.
  • Suppose you are interested in the impact of water pricing on irrigation water demand for farmers in NE. Then, your population is all the farmers in NE.

Important

Population differs depending on the scope of your interest

  • If you are interested in understanding the impact of COVID-19 on child education achievement at the global scale, then your population is every single kid in the world

  • If you are interested in understanding the impact of COVID-19 on child education achievement in U.S., then your population is every single kid in U.S.

Transcript

A sample is the subset of the population you actually get to observe, and it is always a tiny fraction of it. That gap is the entire reason statistics exists. Look at Case 1. You want to know about education and wages, and you have three hundred individuals from each state. Now ask the question in the box honestly. Every state contributes the same three hundred people, so Wyoming and California carry equal weight even though California has roughly seventy times the population. Is that the country? It depends entirely on what you intend to do with it, and that judgement is yours to make, not something the software will make for you.

  • Population: you are interested in the impact of education on wage
  • Sample (example): data on education, income, and many other things for 300 individuals from each State

Question

Is the sample representative of the population?

Transcript

Now the second case, and this one is sharper. Your population is farmers in Nebraska. Your sample is five hundred farmers in the Upper Republican Basin, in the southwest corner of the state. So ask the question again. Those farmers share a climate, a soil type, an aquifer, and a set of water regulations that farmers in eastern Nebraska do not. Whatever you learn about how they respond to water prices is solid for that basin. Extending it to the whole state is a claim you would have to argue for. This is not a reason to throw the data away. It is a reason to be careful about the sentence you write in your abstract.

  • Population: you are interested in the impact of water price on irrigation by farmers in Nebraska
  • Sample (example): data on water price, irrigation water use, and many other things for 500 farmers who farm in the Upper Republican Basin (southwest corner of NE)

Simple univariate model

  • What does \beta_1 measure?

  • Visualized
Transcript

Here is the object the rest of this course is built on. Y equals beta-zero, plus beta-one times x, plus u. Read it as a claim about the population: this is how the world actually works, and we are saying it out loud. Y is what you want to explain, x is what you are explaining it with, and u collects everything else that moves y and that you did not put in the model. The betas are just numbers that give the sentence a size. Now read the callout, because that is the humbling part. You will never see this equation. It belongs to the population, and all you ever get is a sample.

Consider a phenomenon in the population that is correctly represented by the following model ( This is the model you want to learn about using sample ):

\begin{equation} y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x + u \end{equation}
  • y: to be explained by x ( dependent variable)
  • x: explain y ( independent variable , covariate , explanatory variable )
  • u: parts of y that cannot be explained by x ( error term )
  • \beta_0 and \beta_1: real numbers that gives the model a quantitative meaning ( parameters )

Important

You will never know the true model. You can try estimating it using sample! That is what statistics is about.

Transcript

So what does beta-one actually mean? Let’s do the algebra rather than assert it. Write y before, with x in it, and y after, with x plus one in it. Everything else is identical, including u. Subtract the first from the second and everything cancels except beta-one. So beta-one is the change in y that comes from a one-unit change in x. But notice the assumption that did all the work: we held u fixed. That is the phrase in the callout, ceteris paribus, everything else equal. Beta-one is a causal effect only under that condition, and arranging for that condition to hold is what makes this subject hard.

\begin{align} y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x + u \end{align}

If you change x by 1 unit while holding u (everything else) constant,

\begin{align} y_{before} & = \beta_0+\beta_1 x + u \\ y_{after} & = \beta_0+\beta_1 (x + 1) + u \end{align}

The difference in y_{before} and y_{after},

\begin{align} \Delta y = \beta_1 \end{align}

That is, y changes by \beta_1.

So,

  • \beta_1 is the change in y when x increases by 1
  • We call \beta_1 the ceteris paribus (with everything else fixed) causal impact of x on y.
Transcript

Beta-zero is the easy one algebraically. Set x to zero and set u to zero, and the equation collapses to y equals beta-zero, so it is the intercept. Under the zero conditional mean condition introduced later, the average error among observations with x equal to zero is also zero. Only under that condition does beta-zero equal the average value of y at x equal to zero. Now read the note, because this is where the intercept gets oversold. If zero is outside the observed range of x, beta-zero is an extrapolation and may have little useful interpretation. We normally retain an intercept so the model is not unnecessarily constrained to pass through the origin.

\begin{align} y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x + u \end{align}

When x = 0 and u=0,

\begin{align} y=\beta_0 \end{align}

So, \beta_0 represents the intercept.

Under the zero conditional mean condition E[u|x]=0 (see the Crucial condition tab in the next section), \beta_0 = E[y|x=0]: the average value of y among those with x=0.

Note

\beta_0 is most useful when x=0 is substantively meaningful and within the observed range. Otherwise it is an extrapolation. The intercept should normally remain in the model so the regression is not forced through the origin, but it may not deserve a substantive story.

Transcript

The same thing as a picture, and I want you to connect each symbol to something you can see. The blue dot on the vertical axis is beta-zero. It is where the line crosses x equals zero, and here it is one. The red staircase is beta-one. Move one unit to the right along x, and the line rises by two, so beta-one is two. That is all a coefficient is: the rise for a one-unit run. Hold on to this picture, because next lecture we start estimating these two numbers from data, and the whole exercise is deciding where to put this line when the points are scattered around it.


  • \beta_0: intercept
  • \beta_1: coefficient (slope)

Transcript

Let’s switch to an agricultural example, because I want you practising how to spot the error term in the wild. Yield equals beta-zero, plus beta-one times fertilizer, plus u. The question is deliberately open: what is in u? Take a moment and actually list some. Rainfall. Soil organic matter. Drainage. Pest pressure. The farmer’s skill. Whether the field got planted on time. All of it is in there, because u is defined as everything that affects yield and is not fertilizer. Notice how long that list is, and how little of it anybody measures. That is the normal situation, not a pathological one.

Corn yield and fertilizer

\begin{align} yield=\beta_0+\beta_1 fertilizer+u \end{align}

Question

What is in the error term?

Transcript

So here is the position you are in. You do not know beta-zero or beta-one, because those belong to the population. What you have is a stack of observations, a yield and a fertilizer rate for each of n fields. And you want beta-one to be the ceteris paribus effect: what fertilizer does with everything else held fixed. Read the question at the bottom slowly, because it sounds impossible. You are being asked to hold constant a list of things you cannot see. Rainfall, soil, drainage, none of it is in your data. How do you hold fixed what you never measured? The answer is on the next tab, and it is more elegant than you would guess.

\begin{align} yield=\beta_0+\beta_1 fertilizer+u \end{align}
  • you do not know \beta_0 and \beta_1, and would like to estimate them
  • you observe a series of \{yield_i,fertilizer_i\} combinations (i=1,\dots,n)
  • you would like to estimate \beta_1, the impact of fertilizer on yield, ceteris paribus (with everything else fixed)

Question

How could we possibly find the ceteris paribus impact of fertilizer on yield when we do not observe whole bunch of other factors (error term)?

Transcript

Here is the answer, and it is one line: the expected value of u given x is zero. Read the Why block underneath it. You need the conditional average of the unobservables not to change with x, so that when you look at fields with more fertilizer, everything else about them averages out the same as on fields with less. If that holds, differences in expected yield across fertilizer rates trace the regression function. Interpreting that relationship causally also requires x to represent a well-defined intervention and the model to be correctly specified. Remember that merely having zero correlation is weaker, and including an intercept only normalizes the unconditional average error; neither fact by itself establishes zero conditional mean.

It turns out we can identify the ceteris paribus causal impact of x on y as long as the following condition is satisfied:

Zero conditional mean

E(u|x) = 0

Why this is the condition. \beta_1 is a ceteris paribus effect, so it is only recoverable if the rest of the world does not move when x does. E[u|x]=0 says exactly that: whatever value of x you look at, the unobservables average out to the same thing. Any difference in y across values of x is then attributable to x alone.

This is satisfied when E[u|x]=E[u] and the intercept normalization gives E[u]=0. Two distinctions matter:

Important

  • E[u|x]=0 implies that u is uncorrelated with x, but zero correlation alone does not imply zero conditional mean
  • including an intercept can normalize E[u]=0, but it does not make E[u|x]=0 true
Transcript

Now do it yourself, on the model at the top of the screen, with the data described in the middle: two hundred farmers, their yields and their fertilizer rates, in 2023. Two questions. First, what is in u? And read the parenthesis carefully, because only things that actually affect yield count; the farmer’s favourite colour is not in the error term. Second, and this is the one that decides everything, is any of it correlated with fertilizer? Think about how a farmer picks a rate. They look at their soil, their past yields, their expectations for the season. They are choosing fertilizer using the very things sitting inside u. So no, the condition is not satisfied.

Transcript

These next three tabs are the formal versions of what we just said in words. You can follow the course without them, but they are what a referee will expect you to know. Mean independence says the expected value of u given x equals the expected value of u. Verbally, as the first bullet puts it: the average of the error term is the same no matter which value of x you look at, and that common average is just the overall average. It is the precise statement of the intuition from two tabs back. In practice we use it almost interchangeably with saying the error is uncorrelated with x, and the next tab is about how much work “almost” is doing.

Definition: Mean Independence

E[u|x]=E[u]

  • verbally: the average value of the error term (collection of all the unobservables) is the same at any value of x, and that the common average is equal to the average of u over the entire population

  • (almost) interchangeably: the error term is not correlated with x

Transcript

Here is how much work “almost” was doing. Mean independence implies zero correlation, but zero correlation does not imply mean independence. Follow the derivation. The covariance of u and x is the expectation of u times x, minus the product of the two expectations, and since the error averages to zero, that second piece drops out. Then apply the law of iterated expectations: what is left equals the expectation over x of x times the expected value of u given x. If the zero conditional mean condition holds, the inside is zero everywhere, so the whole thing is zero. The reverse fails because u can be uncorrelated with x while still depending on it non-linearly.

Mean independence of u and x implies no correlation. But, no correlation does not imply mean independence.

\begin{aligned} Cov(u,x)= & E[(u-E[u])(x-E[x])] \\\\ = & E[ux]-E[u]E[x]-E[u]E[x]+E[u]E[x]\\\\ = & E[ux]-E[u]E[x] \\\\ = & E[ux] \;\; \text{(since } E[u]=0 \text{)} \\\\ = & E_x\big[x\,E_u[u|x]\big] \;\; \text{(law of iterated expectations)} \end{aligned}

If zero conditional mean condition (E(u|x)=0) is satisfied,

\begin{aligned} Cov(u,x)= & E_x[x \cdot 0] = 0 \end{aligned}
Transcript

The last piece is the claim that the error term averages to zero, which sounds like an assumption you have to earn but is actually free. Suppose it is not zero; suppose u averages to some alpha. Watch the algebra. Add alpha and subtract alpha on the right-hand side. Fold the plus alpha into the intercept and call the result gamma-zero; fold the minus alpha into the error and call it u-two. Now u-two averages to zero by construction, and you have exactly the same model. Nothing was assumed. All it cost is that the intercept quietly absorbed the difference, which is the real reason you always include one.

Expected value of the error term is 0 (E(u)=0).

This is always satisfied as long as an intercept is included in the model:

y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u_1,\;\; \text{where}\;\; E(u_1)=\alpha

Rewriting the model,

\begin{aligned} y & = \beta_0 + \alpha + \beta_1 x + u_1 - \alpha \\\\ & = \gamma_0 + \beta_1 x + u_2 \end{aligned}

where, \gamma_0=\beta_0+\alpha and u_2=u_1-\alpha.

Now, E[u_2]=0.

Transcript

Now we bring the machinery back to the college question we opened with. Here is the model: income equals beta-zero, plus beta-one times College A, plus u. That College A term is a variable equal to one if you attend A and zero if you attend B. It is your first dummy variable, and it makes beta-one literally the difference between the two schools. And u, as always, is everything else, which very much includes ability. So ask the question in the second half of the slide: is the expected value of u, given which college you attend, zero? In plain English, is who ends up at A systematically related to ability?

Income = \beta_0+\beta_1 College\;\; A + u

where College\;\; A is 1 if attending college A, 0 if attending college B, and u is the error term that includes ability.


Zero conditional mean satisfied?

E[u(ability)|college A] = 0?

That is, are attending college A and ability systematically related with each other? Or, is college choice (and acceptance of course) correlated with ability?

Transcript

Look at the figure and answer that question with your eyes. The two horizontal lines are the group averages, and the callout tells you their gap is the forty-one you started with. The two sloped lines are what A and B pay at any given ability, and their gap is twenty. Those are different numbers because the dots are not spread the same way: A’s cloud sits to the right. Higher-ability students got into A and chose A. So ability, sitting inside u, is correlated with which college you attend. The zero conditional mean condition fails, and beta-one from the raw comparison is not the causal effect.

Note

The horizontal lines are the two group averages: their gap is the 40.9 from the table. The sloped lines are the two groups at a given ability: their gap is 20.

Transcript

And here is the counterfactual world, so you can see what success looks like. Same model, but now college choice has nothing to do with ability, and both clouds cover the same range. Look at the horizontal lines. Their gap is now essentially the same as the vertical gap between the sloped lines, which is the true effect of twenty. Read the callout. Nothing about our ignorance changed. We still never measured ability, and it is still sitting in the error term. What changed is that it is no longer correlated with the college variable, so the raw difference finally is the causal effect. This picture is what a randomized experiment buys you.

Important

Both groups now have the same ability distribution, so the gap between the group averages (18) is, up to sampling noise, the same as the 20 gap at any fixed ability. E[u|College\;A]=0 holds, and the raw difference is finally the causal effect.
